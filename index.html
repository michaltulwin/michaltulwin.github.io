Welcome! I am a PhD researcher in Economics at the European University Institute (EUI) in Florence. My advisors are Laurent Mathevet and Giacomo Calzolari.

My research focuses on information economics and game theory, with applications to artificial intelligence, digital markets, and political economy. I combine theoretical work with empirical analysis to study how information and incentives shape strategic behavior.

Before joining the EUI, I worked as a Research Analyst at the World Bank’s Macroeconomics, Trade and Investment Global Practice. I hold an MSc in Applied Economics from the Stockholm School of Economics.

Strategic Disclosure of Evidence Produced In the Market

Michał Tulwin · 2026 · Job market paper

Consumer reviews help buyers assess product quality, but firms may selectively suppress unfavorable feedback or fabricate positive reviews when no purchase takes place. I study how these practices interact with pricing in a two-period monopoly, where genuine reviews arise only when a consumer makes a purchase. The initial price therefore determines both demand and the amount of evidence available to future buyers. Under full pooling, manipulation may raise or lower the price, depending on how it changes the marginal informational value of trade. When the price falls, the resulting increase in trade raises consumer surplus and total welfare. Censorship and fake reviews can generate different, even opposite, price and welfare effects.

Strategic Electoral Mobility

Michał Pawłowski and Michał Tulwin · 2026 · Job market paper

2026 Jacques Rupnik Prize · joint winner

Unequal voting power can give voters an incentive to cast their ballots outside their home constituency. We examine this possibility in Poland’s 2023 parliamentary election, where differences in representation coincided with relatively easy changes of voting location. Combining a theoretical model with municipality-level electoral data, we find evidence of strategic relocations toward districts where votes carry more weight. A counterfactual analysis that equalizes vote power across districts suggests that approximately 33,000 voters relocated strategically under the actual electoral rules. We also find turnout patterns consistent with additional mobilization among local voters in municipalities exposed to these inflows.

Delegating to a Biased AI Agent

Michał Tulwin · 2026

Can the prospect of retraining encourage an AI agent to misrepresent its own bias? In a two-period delegation model, a firm learns about an agent’s privately known bias from its initial behavior and may pay to correct that bias before assigning future tasks. Although retraining can improve alignment, it may also reverse the agent’s incentives: a relatively well-aligned agent may exaggerate its bias to trigger correction and obtain more extensive future delegation. This strategic response creates additional distortions and can eliminate the firm’s expected gains from retraining. In a semi-separating equilibrium, the firm may benefit from committing not to retrain, while higher retraining costs can discourage strategic behavior.
